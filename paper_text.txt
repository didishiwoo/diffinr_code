A B S T R A C T
Reconstructing high-fidelity magnetic resonance (MR) images from under-sampled k-space is a commonly used
strategy to reduce scan time. The posterior sampling of diffusion models based on the real measurement
data holds significant promise of improved reconstruction accuracy. However, traditional posterior sampling
methods often lack effective data consistency guidance, leading to inaccurate and unstable reconstructions.
Implicit neural representation (INR) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for solving inverse problems by
modeling a signal’s attributes as a continuous function of spatial coordinates. In this study, we present a novel
posterior sampler for diffusion models using INR, named DiffINR. The INR-based component incorporates both
the diffusion prior distribution and the MRI physical model to ensure high data fidelity. DiffINR demonstrates
superior performance on in-distribution datasets with remarkable accuracy, even under high acceleration
factors (up to R = 12 in single-channel reconstruction). Furthermore, DiffINR exhibits excellent generalizability
across various tissue contrasts and anatomical structures with low uncertainty. Overall, DiffINR significantly
improves MRI reconstruction in terms of accuracy, generalizability and stability, paving the way for further
accelerating MRI acquisition. Notably, our proposed framework can be a generalizable framework to solve
inverse problems in other medical imaging tasks.
1. Introduction
Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) is a widely used imaging tech-
nique, renowned for its exceptional soft tissue contrast, yet hampered
by the inherently long scan time. A common strategy to accelerate
MRI scans involves under-sampling k-space data while maintaining
the diagnostic quality of the reconstructed images. This approach is
challenging because image reconstruction from sparsely sampled data
is an ill-posed inverse problem, making it difficult to achieve artifact-
free reconstructions. Extensive research has explored various methods
to address this challenge, including traditional optimization and deep
learning (DL)-based approaches (Lustig et al., 2007; Aggarwal et al.,
2018). Traditional methods, such as compressed sensing (CS), leverage
prior knowledge about the inherent sparsity in a transformational
domain Lustig et al. (2007), Doneva et al. (2010), Gu et al. (2021) or

rely on low-rankness (Jin et al., 2016; Lingala et al., 2011; Zhang et al.,
2015) to reconstruct images. However, these methods face limitations
when it comes to achieving higher acceleration rates. Such limitations
stem from the assumptions regarding the prior knowledge that is typi-
cally either manually constructed or confined to learned sparse codes,
limiting their efficacy and adaptability.
Over the past few years, supervised DL has been regarded as a
powerful data-driven approach for solving inverse problems of MRI
reconstruction (Aggarwal et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2018; Hammernik
et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2017; Ongie et al., 2020), demonstrating ad-
vanced performance in reconstructing highly under-sampled MRI data.
However, these methods need large quantities of high-quality data pairs
for training, which is a challenge to obtain in the medical imaging
field. Moreover, the effectiveness of these models tends to decline

when applied to out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, such as differ-

To overcome these challenges, data-specific unsupervised DL methods
have been proposed, such as deep image prior (DIP). The DIP frame-
work proposed in Ulyanov et al. (2018) shows that convolutional neural
networks (CNNs) have the intrinsic ability to regularize a variety of
ill-posed inverse problems without the need for paired training data.
However, DIP-based methods often suffer from spectral bias (Rahaman
et al., 2019; Shi et al., 2022), leading to the loss of high-frequency
details in reconstructed images. To address this limitation, coordinate-
based implicit neural representation (INR) has emerged as an effective
solution (Yüce et al., 2022). Technically, INR represents the target
object as a continuous function of the spatial coordinates, which is
parameterized with a fully connected neural network. Before being
fed into the network, the coordinates are transformed through unique
encoding techniques into a more resilient and adaptable representation
domain (e.g., Fourier domain Tancik et al. (2020)) to reduce spectral
bias (Mildenhall et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). A well-trained INR
acts as a continuous function to map image spatial coordinates to
corresponding image intensities. INR offers several notable advantages:
(1) a flexible framework for incorporating specific priors or regular-
izations, and (2) the ability to capture high-frequency details of the
representing object (Tancik et al., 2020; Müller et al., 2022). Previous
studies have shown that INR can outperform traditional methods when
combined with specific imaging physical priors (Shen et al., 2022; Feng
et al., 2023). However, similar to the traditional CS-based methods,
INR faces a limitation in high acceleration rates due to its reliance
on hand-crafted priors, such as total variation (TV) (Strong and Chan,
2003). These hand-crafted priors often struggle to represent complex
underlying data distributions accurately.
Recently, the diffusion model has shown its superior capability in
MRI acceleration (Chung et al., 2022b; Song et al., 2024). Specifically,
an unconditional pre-trained diffusion model serves as a generative
prior and contains rich knowledge of the complicated MR image struc-
tures independent of specific scanning parameters or tissue types. By
leveraging these unconditional diffusion priors, reconstructing specific
MR images with under-sampled data to ensure data consistency (DC)
can be conceptualized as posterior sampling for diffusion models. Cur-
rently, there are two main categories of posterior sampling methods for
DC operations: (1) projecting the generated prior onto the measurement
space at each reverse sampling step (Song et al., 2021; Peng et al.,
2022; Chung and Ye, 2022; Jalal et al., 2021) and (2) calculating
the posterior probability from the measurements based on certain
assumptions (Chung et al., 2022a, 2023a). Although these two types of
posterior sampling methods have demonstrated commendable efficacy
in MRI reconstruction, they encounter challenges with uncertainty and
insufficient DC (Chung et al., 2023b), resulting in inaccurate and in-
stable MRI reconstruction. This is especially problematic when dealing
with highly accelerated rates or OOD data, where these posterior sam-
pling methods are prone to producing erroneous and unstable results
(e.g., ring artifacts (Chung and Ye, 2022)). In clinical settings, any
inaccuracies and instabilities in image reconstructions could lead to
misjudgments by healthcare professionals.
In this study, we develop an INR-based posterior sampling technique
for high-acceleration MRI reconstruction called DiffINR. Inspired by the
work in Chung and Ye (2022), Chung et al. (2023a), we introduce
INR into the posterior sampling process of diffusion models for DC
operation. Our INR-based posterior sampler maintains DC throughout
the diffusion sampling process by leveraging INR’s unique ability to
integrate diffusion priors with the MRI physical models. Specifically, we
use a well-trained diffusion model to generate prior information about
high-quality MRI distribution. We then employ INR to guarantee the
reconstruction DC for specific measurements. We conduct experiments
on in-distribution and out-of-distribution datasets to demonstrate the
superiority of the proposed method. Results show that the INR-based
posterior sampler can generate more accurate and stable reconstruction
results. Our contributions are as follows:
(1) To the best of our knowledge, DiffINR is the first method that
explores the potential of combining the diffusion model with
INR-based DC operations for solving the inverse problem in MRI
reconstruction.
(2) Our INR-based module is training-free and can be easily inte-
grated into other diffusion models. Additionally, the INR-based
module can be easily combined with specific physical models
(e.g., MRI forward physical model) to achieve accurate and
stable DC.
(3) The quantitative and qualitative results demonstrate that DiffINR
effectively improves the accuracy of MRI reconstruction on in-
distribution datasets and offers performance comparable to su-
pervised DL methods. In addition, DiffINR exhibits superior
performance in OOD datasets, indicating its excellent general-
izability.
(4) DiffINR considerably reduces the uncertainty typically seen in
diffusion-based MRI reconstruction, yielding high-quality results
with high stability even when working with noisy measure-
ments.

2.2. Diffusion model in MRI reconstruction
In recent years, diffusion models have shown great potential in
solving the MAP estimation of MRI reconstruction for better accuracy
and generalization capability (Jiang et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2023;
Korkmaz et al., 2023; Xie and Li, 2022; Cao et al., 2022b). Jalal et al.
(2021) first proposed to use of annealed Langevin Dynamics posterior
sampling for multi-channel acquired MRI reconstruction. Chung and Ye
(2022) and Song et al. (2021) used score-based diffusion with iterative
DC projections, achieving high-quality posterior sampling results. Based
on the idea of DC projection in the sampling phase, Peng et al. (2022)
proposed a coarse-to-fine sampling strategy for accelerating MRI recon-
struction. Güngör et al. (2023) introduced the prior adaption operation
in diffusion models for fast and reliable reconstructions.
To further improve the reconstruction performance, Cao et al.
(2024) proposed a high-frequency space diffusion model to improve

2.3. Implicit neural representation
The concept of INR first emerged in the context of view synthesis in
computer vision. Mildenhall et al. (2021) pioneered the utilization of a
fully connected network to encode a 3D scene as a neural radiance field
(NeRF). Researchers have explored the fusion of INR with various co-
ordinate encoding functions, enabling the capturing of high-frequency
image details, such as the Fourier feature map (Tancik et al., 2020),
radial encoding (Liu et al., 2022), and hash encoding (Müller et al.,
2022). Leveraging multilayer perceptron (MLP) and an appropriate
encoding function that maps input coordinates to a high-dimensional
space, INR has showcased remarkable efficacy across diverse computer
vision tasks (Park et al., 2019; Saragadam et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2023).
Previous work demonstrates INR’s notable capability in addressing
inverse problems in medical image reconstruction (Feng et al., 2023;
Wu et al., 2023; Reed et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2023). For MRI acceler-
ation, Shen et al. (2022) proposed INR learning with prior embedding
(NeRP) to get a high-quality result, while it is limited to the case
where long-term longitudinal scanning is performed. Similarly, Feng
et al. (2023) proposed an INR approach with sensitivity map estimation
(IMJENSE) for parallel MRI. Although IMJENSE demonstrated rela-
tively high acceleration rates compared to traditional methods, it faces
limitations when dealing with highly under-sampled data due to a lack
of comprehensive prior knowledge of the complex data distributions.

3.3. INR guided posterior sampling
To solve the DC problem in Eq. (12), currently there are two strate-
gies, which are direct DC projection and diffusion posterior sampling
(DPS). Score-MRI is a representative DC projection method that tries
to directly project the noise image 𝑥𝑡−1 to the measurement space.
However, there is a relatively large error in direct projection methods
because of the inaccurate forward acquisition model between 𝑥𝑡−1 and
𝑦, which makes projection-based methods easily getting to the local
minimum and sensitive to the noise perturbations. Additionally, DPS
has a high uncertainty due to insufficient DC constraint, which is also
called the soft DC operation (Song et al., 2024). Thus, the limitations of
current DC operators might lead to an unstable and biased estimation.
In this study, we propose to use INR to guide the DC operation
during posterior sampling with a two-stage learning strategy. The
overview of proposed INR posterior sampling strategy is shown in
Fig. 1. Specifically, INR learns the image 𝑥0|𝑡−1 to embed the prior infor-
mation into the network. Then, we refine 𝑥0|𝑡−1 based on measurement
data to ensure data fidelity. In this way, we are able to enhance the
intermediate 𝑥𝑡−1 for DC through iterative refinement of the parameters
within the INR network, rather than tuning the update step size in
DPS. Leveraging the powerful representation capability of INR (Feng
et al., 2023), we can achieve a highly faithful refinement result 𝑥0|𝑡−1
for specific scanning protocols. Detailed explanations of the two-stage
INR learning are outlined below.

Our INR architecture consists of a tiny MLP that includes one input
layer, two hidden layers, and one output layer. Each of the hidden
layers has 64 neurons, and all layers, except for the final one, utilize the
ReLU activation function. Functionally, we deploy two separate MLPs
to represent the real and imaginary components of the MRI images.
We incorporate hash encoding to effectively represent the features of
the input coordinates. The hyperparameters of our hash encoding are
following the default parameters in Shi et al. (2022). In the prior
embedding stage, the number of iterations (𝑁𝑝𝑟) is 250 and the learning
rate is 1 × 10−3. In the DC refinement stage, the number of iterations
is 250 and the learning rate is 1 × 10−5. All the hyperparameters are
trained with the Adam optimizer (Kingma and Ba, 2014).
All the experiments are implemented using PyTorch 1.11.0 in
Python 3.7 on a workstation with an Intel i7-12700F processor, 32 GB
RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090GPU with 24 GB memory.
Note that all hyper-parameters are tuned on five slices of two scans
from the fastMRI knee dataset in one single-channel reconstruction task
(Gaussian 1D R = 8, ACS = 12) and are maintained constantly across
all other samples.
4. Experiments and results
4.1. Datasets
(1) In-distribution Dataset
Our in-distribution experiments were conducted using the fastMRI
knee dataset (Knoll et al., 2020; Zbontar et al., 2018). We obtained
all the fastMRI datasets from the NYU fastMRI Initiative database with
approval from the New York University School of Medicine Institutional
Review Board. The validation knee dataset contains 199 proton density
(PD)/proton density fat suppression (PDFS) volumes. Specifically, the
knee data was acquired using a 2D turbo spin-echo sequence with a ma-
trix size of 320 × 320. For single-channel reconstruction, we randomly
chose 40 slices from 15 volumes. For multi-channel (15 channels) coil
reconstruction, we randomly chose 20 slices from 8 volumes.
(2) Out-of-distribution Dataset
To evaluate the generalizability of DiffINR, a subject’s knee was
scanned using a 5.0T scanner (uMR Jupiter, United Image Health
care, Shanghai, China) equipped with a 24-channel knee receiver coil.
The experiment was approved by the Shanghai Jiao Tong University
Human Ethics Committee and the informed consent was signed before
scanning. We used a 2D fast spin echo (FSE) sequence to obtain T1
weighted (T1w) images. The parameters are: matrix size = 896 × 896 × 26,
voxel size = 0.18 × 0.18 × 3.3 mm3, TE = 8.92 ms, TR = 610 ms, flip angle
= 110◦. We randomly selected 10 slices for quantitative analysis.
To explore a more challenging scenario, we further conducted ex-
periments on the fastMRI brain dataset. The brain data was scanned
using a fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequence with 16
channels. We randomly selected 20 brain slices from 5 volumes for
evaluation.
Our data preprocessing and the generation of under-sampling masks
followed the methodology described in Chung and Ye (2022). All data
were initially scaled and cropped to a size of 320 × 320 to align with the
pre-trained diffusion model, ensuring optimal reconstruction results.
Our experiments primarily employed two types of masks: 1D and 2D.
The 1D mask samples in a strip format, with full sampling in the central
region, known as the auto-calibration signal (ACS) region, and under-
sampling in the remaining areas according to the acceleration factor
and specific sampling function. The 2D sampling pattern is depicted as
a dot matrix, where the sampling procedure spans the entire k-space
based on the acceleration factor and distribution function.
4.2. Performance evaluation
DiffINR was compared with the following previous algorithms: (1)
scan-specific methods P-LORAKS (Haldar and Zhuo, 2016; Kim and
Haldar, 2018), and IMJENSE (Feng et al., 2023), (2) supervised DL
methods MoDL (Aggarwal et al., 2018) and ReconFormer (Guo et al.,
2024), and (3) diffusion-based methods Score-MRI (Chung and Ye,
2022), and DPS (Chung et al., 2023a). Specifically, P-LORAKS is a
conventional calibration-less method. IMJENSE is a scan-specific INR-
based method. ReconFormer is an end-to-end DL method for single-
channel MRI data reconstruction. MoDL is a supervised model-based
method for multi-channel reconstruction. The two supervised DL meth-
ods were retrained using the fastMRI single-channel and multi-channel
datasets, respectively. Specifically, we used randomly selected 8000
slices from 400 scans in the fastMRI knee dataset for training. We
trained specific models under each undersampling pattern for the su-
pervised methods. The diffusion-based methods compared in our study
are all posterior sampling methods. For a fair comparison, we used
the pre-trained diffusion model provided by Chung and Ye (2022) to
estimate the scores for all the posterior sampling methods and used
pre-estimated sensitivity maps via ESPIRiT (Uecker et al., 2014) for
all the multi-channel reconstruction tasks. The inputs for P-LORAKS
are under-sampled k-space images and the inputs of IMJENSE are the
spatial coordinates of the image. The inputs are under-sampled MRI
slices for both the supervised and diffusion-based methods. We com-
puted peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity index
measure (SSIM) for quantitative evaluation. Moreover, we calculated
uncertainty maps for diffusion-based posterior sampling methods to
evaluate their stability.
To demonstrate the superiority of our method, we conducted six
experiments. (1) We tested the performance of DiffINR and com-
pared methods on the single-channel and multi-channel fastMRI knee
datasets. (2) To demonstrate the generalizability of DiffINR, OOD
datasets with various acquisition parameters and tissue structures were
tested. (3) To verify the stability of our INR-based posterior sampler,
we calculated the uncertainty of diffusion-based methods and assessed
their reconstruction performance in noisy conditions. (4) We designed
ablation studies to illustrate the effectiveness of selected hyperparame-
ters. (5) To illustrate the superiority of the INR-based posterior sampler,
we compared the performance of DIP and INR for guided diffusion
posterior sampling with acceleration ratios ranging from R = 2 to R
= 8. (6) Taking inference time into consideration, we tested diffusion-
based methods with decreasing sampling steps, assessing the trade-off
between inference time and reconstruction quality.

4.4. Comparison results of out-of-distribution datasets
In Chung and Ye (2022), the authors demonstrated the strong
generalizability of diffusion models in MRI reconstructions. However,
they noted that reconstructions often displayed artifacts under OOD
conditions. These artifacts may arise from insufficient DC refinement,
which limits the generalizability of diffusion models. To assess the
performance of our INR-based posterior sampler under OOD conditions,
we applied our method to T1w images of human knee acquired at
5T and brain FLAIR images from the fastMRI dataset. Fig. 4 and
Table 2 present the qualitative and quantitative OOD results by dif-
ferent methods at a Random 1D accelerated factor of R = 6 with

the supervised method, MoDL, significantly deteriorated, particularly
in brain reconstruction, as shown in Fig. 4(b). This decline can be
attributed to the limited generalizability of supervised DL methods (Lin
et al., 2021; Sandino et al., 2020). In contrast, diffusion-based methods
outperformed the supervised method in fastMRI brain data, which is
notably different from the original training dataset of knee images.
Overall, our INR-based posterior sampler demonstrates considerable
advantages in OOD scenarios compared to other sampling methods.

4.5. Stability evaluation for reconstruction results
(1) Uncertainty Quantification
Due to the inherent stochasticity in the diffusion model generation
process, the reconstruction results may contain intrinsic biases. To
assess the reliability and precision of our method in clinical settings, we
quantified the uncertainties associated with diffusion-based methods
at different acceleration rates using single-channel data. We generated
uncertainty maps by calculating the variance of multiple parallel re-
constructions, following the approach outlined in Chung and Ye (2022).
Specifically, we calculated the mean values and the pixel-wise standard
deviations from eight concurrent generative reconstructions. Fig. 5
displays the uncertainty variations of diffusion-based methods as the
acceleration rates increase. From R = 2 to R = 6, there was a notable
rise in uncertainty values for Score-MRI and DPS, with the DPS method
exhibiting a larger variance. In comparison, our method maintained the
lowest uncertainty, even under conditions of high under-sampling rates.
(2) Noisy Reconstruction
To assess the susceptibility to noise of diffusion-based methods, we
introduced varying levels of additive white Gaussian noise into the
real and imaginary components of under-sampled k-space data, yielding
images with SNR of 32 dB and 40 dB, respectively. As shown in Fig. 6(a)
and (b), Score-MRI and DPS exhibit deviations in the details of tissue
edge compared to the ground truth image. However, these structural er-
rors and artifacts are significantly reduced in DiffINR’s reconstructions,
indicating that our method has superior noise resilience compared to
other posterior sampling methods.
4.6. Effectiveness of the hyperparameters
(1) INR Interval (𝑘)
The parameter 𝑘determines the frequency of the DC operation. If
the interval is set too small, the inference time will increase propor-
tionally. Conversely, if the interval is set too large, the number of DC
operations will decrease, which can result in an increased DC loss. We
tested six representative 𝑘values (10, 25, 50, 75, 100, 200) on the
single-channel knee dataset with 1D Gaussian sampling (R = 8, ACS
= 12). Fig. 7(a) displays the visual results of different 𝑘values. In
Fig. 7(b), we observed that SSIM and PSNR drop dramatically when
𝑘 > 50. Therefore, we empirically chose 𝑘= 50 as the optimal trade-off
between inference time and image quality.
(2) INR Start Timestep (𝑡∗)
In our experiments, we observed that our INR does not yield uni-
form effects at each time step. This phenomenon aligns with previous
studies referenced in Song et al. (2024), Yu et al. (2023). During
the early stages of diffusion sampling, the prior image 𝑥0|𝑡contains
substantial noise and does not offer sufficient distribution information
for the INR. To address this, instead of introducing the INR at the
very beginning of the sampling process, we set a start timestep 𝑡∗. This
strategy not only shortens inference time but also ensures a reasonably
high level of DC loss for INR prior guidance (Güngör et al., 2023). In
Fig. 8(a), we presented the quantification results of a single sample with
different 𝑡∗, with 𝑘= 50 and 𝑇= 2000. From the curve in Fig. 8(a), we
observed that there is no significant variation in the performance of
DiffINR when 𝑡∗> 1200, but a notable decrease when 𝑡∗< 1200. Thus,
we selected 𝑡∗= 1200 as INR start timestep in all our experiments.
(3) Number of Prior Embedding Steps (𝑁𝑝𝑟)
In Fig. 8(b), we assessed the effectiveness of 𝑁𝑝𝑟by learning an
intermediate diffusion prior image at 𝑡= 300. Given the tendency of
INR to overfit, the basic idea of selecting 𝑁𝑝𝑟is as large as possible
to guarantee the similarity between the diffusion prior image and the
INR output image. In Fig. 8(b), after 250 epochs, DiffINR fully captured
the prior information generated by the diffusion model. The PSNR and
SSIM values in Fig. 8(b) were calculated between the diffusion prior
image and the INR output.

5. Conclusion
In this study, we proposed DiffINR, an INR-based diffusion posterior
sampler for highly accelerated MRI reconstruction. We introduced a
pre-trained diffusion model to generate noiseless prior images, allowing
the INR to learn high-quality distribution information. By incorporating
the physical model into the INR, robust DC is realized. Through itera-
tive INR procedures, the diffusion model produces highly accurate and
stable reconstruction results. Experimental results on in-distribution
and OOD datasets show that DiffINR outperforms the compared meth-
ods. The results from OOD datasets and uncertainty quantification
further demonstrate DiffINR’s strong generalizability and stability. Ad-
ditionally, this posterior sampler is training-free and is not limited
by the specific diffusion model, facilitating its integration with other
pre-trained diffusion models for task-specific reconstruction. In sum-
mary, DiffINR delivers high accuracy, excellent generalizability, and
stability, positioning it a promising solution for highly accelerated MRI
reconstruction.
